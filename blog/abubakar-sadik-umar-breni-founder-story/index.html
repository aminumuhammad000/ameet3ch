Introduction

What if education were designed around how people actually learn, rather than expecting every student to follow the same rigid path?

That question lies at the heart of the vision discussed by Abubakar Sadik Umar, founder or co-founder of BRENi, in an in-depth interview with Fagen Hikima.

Rather than accepting the conventional one-size-fits-all approach to education, Umar describes a different direction—one informed by cognitive science and an ambition to make learning genuinely personalised.

Yet his story is not only about education technology. It is also an unvarnished account of the uncertainty inherent in building a startup, the immense personal sacrifices founders make, the non-negotiable importance of choosing the right partners, and the conviction required to persevere when others may not yet grasp the vision.

His experience offers rich, practical lessons for aspiring entrepreneurs who want to build something transformative in Nigeria and across Africa.

1. The Problem with a One-Size-Fits-All Approach to Education

One of the central themes in Umar's discussion is his criticism of conventional education models that expect learners with wildly different needs, baselines, and aptitudes to follow identical curricula at identical paces.

As reflected in the interview, he views this one-size-fits-all model as a fundamental bottleneck of traditional education systems worldwide.

Students do not learn at the same pace, respond to identical teaching modalities, or enter classrooms with uniform prerequisites. A rigid system that treats everyone identically frequently fails to provide an effective, engaging learning experience for individuals on either side of the average curve.

For Umar, this disparity creates an urgent imperative to rethink how education is architected and delivered from first principles.

The Digitisation Trap

Moving education online does not automatically make it personalised. A platform can deliver recorded video lectures through a laptop or smartphone while retaining the exact same systemic limitations as a physical lecture hall. The fundamental challenge is whether technology can build a dynamic feedback loop that responds to the unique learner.

Instead of simply reproducing traditional pedagogical methods in digital form, the ambition behind BRENi is to develop a genuinely individualised approach to learning.

2. Why BRENi Is Exploring a Different Approach to Learning

During the conversation with Fagen Hikima, Umar highlights cognitive science as a foundational pillar of BRENi's educational philosophy.

Cognitive science studies how the human mind thinks, absorbs information, recalls memory, solves problems, and develops mastery. Its application to educational technology moves beyond mere "content hosting" to focus on the neurological and psychological processes that facilitate retention and comprehension.

Umar also discusses transcending static, pre-recorded lecture formats. One of the central concepts he uses to articulate BRENi's product direction is building an avatar for the learner.

In the context of his explanation, a learner avatar represents a personalized digital model of the student's cognitive state—adapting challenges, pacing, and feedback to their evolving comprehension.

Traditional EdTech vs. Cognitive-First Learning

Traditional Digital Video

Static 45-minute video lectures, uniform progression, passive listening, and one-way instruction with high drop-off rates.

Cognitive Avatar Model

Interactive, adaptive pacing matched to learner cognition, immediate conceptual feedback loops, and individualised progression.

However, as responsible editorial practice demands, it is worth noting that the interview introduces this concept at a visionary level. Specific technical implementations and software architecture should be independently confirmed as BRENi rolls out its production software.

Nevertheless, the ambition raises an indispensable question for founders: are you merely digitising existing systems, or are you architecting a measurably superior experience?

3. Understanding How People Consume Information

Another compelling dimension of Umar's discussion is his reference to modern social platforms such as TikTok and the dramatic shift in how contemporary learners consume media.

Modern audiences routinely consume information through micro-videos, rich visual metaphors, and high-cadence digital formats. For educational businesses, understanding these behavioral patterns is essential for designing interfaces that don't immediately induce friction.

BRENi's attention to social platform consumption reflects an acute awareness of user attention spans. However, Umar and experienced educators equally recognize a crucial distinction:

"Do not confuse content distribution with learning effectiveness. A format that captures attention does not automatically produce deep comprehension or long-term recall."

The overarching engineering challenge for modern edtech companies is synthesizing engaging, bite-sized delivery with rigorous learning architectures that build genuine intellectual mastery over time.

For entrepreneurs in any vertical, the takeaway is clean: deeply understand how your users behave in daily life, but never substitute viral engagement metrics for core value delivery.

4. The Personal Sacrifices Behind Building a Startup

Building a groundbreaking startup requires founders to commit personal capital, time, emotional stamina, and attention long before market validation is guaranteed.

In the interview, Umar discusses putting his own personal resources on the line to sustain BRENi through its exploratory and developmental stages. His candor highlights the financial vulnerability and high-stakes risk that founders routinely navigate behind closed doors.

Beyond finances, he describes the friction of challenging established educational conventions. When you propose an alternative to century-old institutional habits, scepticism is the default reaction.

This reflects a phase of entrepreneurship often romanticized in retrospect but excruciating in the moment: the long gestation period where you have conviction in a solution, but have not yet demonstrated product-market fit at scale.

Balancing Conviction with Prudence

Conviction is mandatory, but blind conviction can be lethal. Sustainable founders balance strong belief in their mission with ruthless financial discipline, customer feedback, and the agility to discard assumptions when confronted with hard data.

5. Why the Right Co-Founder Matters

Among the most emphatic lessons from Umar's interview is his perspective on co-founder selection and team composition.

He reflects deeply on balancing technical intelligence with underlying character. A prospective partner may boast elite programming skills or an impressive resume, but technical brilliance without integrity or aligned incentives will disintegrate a startup during stressful cycles.

In the interview, Umar explicitly cautions against choosing brilliant but selfish partners, underscoring that greed, ego, and lack of alignment are fatal liabilities in a founding team.

5 Critical Questions Before Choosing a Co-Founder

Use this evaluation criteria inspired by Umar's experience before formalizing any startup partnership:

  • 1
    Shared Vision: Do you agree on the fundamental destination, target audience, and moral non-negotiables of the business?
  • 2
    Constructive Conflict: Can you disagree vigorously on product or strategy without taking offense or letting personal egos damage trust?
  • 3
    Equity & Compensation Clarity: Are vesting schedules, roles, time commitments, and financial expectations documented clearly upfront?
  • 4
    Integrity Under Pressure: When cash is tight and deadlines loom, will your partner put the venture's survival ahead of personal short-term gains?
  • 5
    Complementary Competencies: Do your strengths cover their blind spots (e.g., product architecture vs. commercial distribution)?

A durable founding partnership does not demand identical personalities or mirror skills. It demands complementary capabilities anchored by mutual respect, radical transparency, and shared moral standards.

6. The Personal Relationships Behind Entrepreneurial Success

One of the most candid moments of Umar's discussion with Fagen Hikima came when he addressed the domestic reality of entrepreneurship:

"Your wife can make you or break you."

This striking observation encapsulates a reality often omitted from startup conferences: a founder's personal life directly impacts their capacity to lead and endure.

Early-stage ventures inevitably impose acute pressures on family routines, household finances, emotional availability, and personal time. A supportive spouse and stable domestic partnership act as an emotional shock absorber during brutal business setbacks.

Conversely, unresolved domestic friction or competing lifestyle expectations can paralyze an entrepreneur's mental resilience when clear decision-making is most critical.

While Umar specifically spoke of marriage, the universal lesson applies across all core personal bonds: entrepreneurs must maintain honest communication with loved ones, align expectations early, and safeguard their domestic foundation.

7. Startup Life Is a Roller Coaster

When summarizing the entrepreneurial experience, Umar utilizes a familiar metaphor: startup life is a roller coaster.

He shares unflinching confidence that the team intends to build BRENi into a landmark technology startup, while openly recognizing the acute volatility of the journey.

Building an enterprise rarely follows an orderly upward trajectory. One month may deliver technical breakthroughs and enthusiastic beta testers, followed immediately by technical debt, fundraising delays, or unexpected platform challenges.

Redefining Resilience

True resilience is not merely stubborn endurance or refusing to admit mistakes. Genuine resilience is active learning: diagnosing bottlenecks early, letting go of unworkable features, and recalibrating execution while staying relentlessly focused on the core problem.

For entrepreneurs building in Nigeria—where foreign exchange fluctuations, power infrastructure, and payment friction present constant friction—this blend of psychological grit and strategic flexibility is vital for long-term survival.

8. What Aspiring Nigerian Entrepreneurs Can Learn from BRENi

Synthesizing Umar's interview yields six tactical principles for founders building tech-enabled businesses in emerging markets:

Principle Founder Focus Practical Action for Nigerian Founders
1. Start with Real Problems Identify structural friction, not passing hype Validate if target users currently experience pain and have willingness to adopt or pay for a fix.
2. Challenge Conventions Rethink delivery rather than merely copying Don't simply digitise broken workflows; ask what 10x value a native digital experience creates.
3. Vet Co-Founders for Character Prioritise trust and maturity over sheer ego Execute formal co-founder agreements, clear equity vesting milestones, and trial sprint projects.
4. Prudent Capital Management Protect personal and venture runway Bootstrap leanly, control fixed overheads, and establish unambiguous kill/pivot metrics.
5. Stay Data-Informed Maintain mission belief while testing assumptions Continuously listen to early user metrics, watch completion drop-offs, and refine user loops.
6. Ecosystem Thinking Leave a blueprint for the next generation Document decisions openly, mentor emerging builders, and strengthen local tech communities.

9. Building a Legacy Beyond the Business

Towards the conclusion of the interview, Umar emphasizes an aspiration that transcends the commercial metrics of software: creating an institutional blueprint for the next generation of builders.

Entrepreneurial ecosystems do not mature solely through venture capital infusions. They evolve when founding teams openly articulate the hard-won insights, failures, and operational frameworks encountered along the journey.

When founders speak candidly about partner conflict, personal doubt, capital allocation, and pedagogical models, they demystify business creation for younger technologists across Northern Nigeria and the wider African continent.

Whether BRENi fully realizes its lofty ambitions will be determined by execution, product iteration, and market response. But the underlying ethos is undeniably noble: building solutions that elevate human capability while mentoring those who will follow.

Conclusion: Build with Conviction, but Learn from Reality

Abubakar Sadik Umar's journey with BRENi illustrates both the thrilling promise and the sobering difficulties of building technology in Africa.

His reflections underline that impactful innovation is never just about writing code or producing digital content. It demands enduring character, respectful co-founding dynamics, family support, and an unshakeable commitment to solving real human problems.

For every aspiring founder, student, and tech leader watching this story, the ultimate lesson is this: dare to rethink conventional systems, surround yourself with people of character, manage your runway with wisdom, and never lose your appetite to learn from reality.
